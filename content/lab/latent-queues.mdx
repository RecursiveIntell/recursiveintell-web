## Experiment

Modern distributed systems communicate through message queues—Kafka, SQS, RabbitMQ. When one queue backs up, downstream services feel the pressure, but the root cause is often invisible until it's too late.

This experiment builds a visualization that shows queue latency propagation in real-time. Think of it as a weather map for your async infrastructure.

## The Insight

Queue depth alone is misleading. A queue with 10,000 messages might be fine (high throughput, fast processing) while a queue with 100 messages might be a crisis (stalled consumer, growing latency).

What matters is **latent time**: how long messages sit in the queue before processing. When latent time compounds across multiple queues in a dependency chain, you get cascading delays that are hard to debug.

## Implementation

The visualization connects to queue metrics (currently Kafka and SQS) and builds a directed graph of data flow. Each edge shows:

- Current queue depth
- P50 and P99 latent time
- Processing rate (messages/second)
- Trend direction (improving, stable, degrading)

The graph uses force-directed layout to cluster related services, with edge thickness representing message volume and color indicating health (green → yellow → red based on latent time thresholds).

## Technical Details

- **Data collection**: Prometheus queries via PromQL, aggregated at 10-second intervals
- **Graph layout**: D3-force with custom collision detection for labels
- **Backend**: Node.js WebSocket server that pushes updates to connected clients
- **Frontend**: React with SVG rendering for smooth animations

## Findings

After running this on our production Kafka cluster:

1. **Cascade patterns are predictable** — Most incidents follow the same propagation paths. Once you see these paths, you can set targeted alerts.

2. **Small queues cause big problems** — The queues that caused incidents weren't the busiest ones; they were small queues with bursty traffic patterns and no autoscaling.

3. **Time-of-day effects are dramatic** — Queue health varies significantly by hour. A dashboard that looks fine at 2 PM might be screaming at 10 AM.
